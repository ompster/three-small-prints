i. The origin

Out of a small drawer of trial and error.

Our twins were born at twenty-four weeks. One of them, Eloise, got quite sick in NICU and lost a large length of her bowel to resection. She came home a long time later with short gut syndrome, a central venous access device for nightly TPN, and a PEG tube for overnight Pepti Junior feeds. The routine settles you in fast.

None of the prints on this page are clever, and they were not designed to be. They are the small fixes I made for the parts of the setup that kept catching me out. The hook because there was nowhere safe to hang the bag in the bedroom. The stand because ampules roll, and my eyesight has never been the kind that finds tiny things at 2am. The practice ampule because I cracked the first real one with too much enthusiasm, and the cut on my thumb spent the next fortnight reminding me about it.

Our TPN nurse now hands a small set to families being discharged. If you have ended up with one, this page is what each piece is for, and why I bothered.

no. 1 · The Hook

The S-hook for the bag.

An S-shape that hangs the bag off a wardrobe rail. Top loop on the rail, bottom loop on the bag handle. That is the whole idea.

Keeps the bag off radiators, windowsills, and anything else you would rather not store a litre of liquid nutrition on top of. Useful between fridge storage and hang time, and a tidy spot to park the bag while you set up the rest.

On use Slide the rail through the top opening rather than trying to clip it on. Once it is on, it stays put even if the bag does a small impression of a pendulum.
no. 2 · The Stand

A weighted base for the ampule.

A wide dome with a hole in the top, sized for a glass ampule or a turalock cap. The wide bottom is the whole point. It does not tip over on you.

Helpful at 2am, with bad lighting, with eyesight that has seen better decades, or any time you have watched an ampule roll quietly across the bench and silently judged the design choices of glass cylinders. Holds the thing firmly enough that you can use both hands on the syringe instead of one to stop the ampule moving.

On use Slide the ampule into the centre hole until it sits snug. If the stand moves when you press the syringe in, something has gone wrong, or the cat is involved.
no. 3 · The Practice Ampule

A plastic dummy for the snap.

PLA, printed to roughly match the snap force of real glass. Use it to practice the technique before doing it for real, at 11pm on a Tuesday, with no skin to spare.

Most people get their first real ampule wrong in one of two ways. Too gentle, and nothing happens. Too enthusiastic, and you find out the inside of a snapped ampule is sharp. I went with option two. With one of these, you can snap it, bin it, print another, and quietly build the muscle memory: the angle, the pressure, and getting used to the sound without flinching.

On use Two fingers each side of the neck, thumbs together, push outward with even pressure. The snap is sharper than you expect. It always is.
